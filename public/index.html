01 Study / Follow one signal

An SDR does not “listen to a frequency.” It captures a slice of spectrum, converts it to complex baseband, and hands you a stream of numbers. The RF chain is the path between the antenna and those numbers.

Antenna→Filter / LNA→Mixer + LO→ADC→DDC / decimation→Host: I/Q → DSP → bits
  • Antenna converts an electromagnetic field into a tiny voltage. Resonance and polarization matter more than marketing gain figures.
  • Filter and LNA select a band and amplify it. The LNA adds noise but can also be overloaded by a strong nearby signal.
  • Mixer and local oscillator translate RF to baseband: f_baseband = f_rf − f_LO. Negative baseband frequencies are real and useful.
  • ADC samples and quantizes the signal. More bits means more theoretical dynamic range; more samples per second means more bandwidth.
  • DDC and decimation select a channel and reduce the rate before the host. This is where “wideband capture” becomes a manageable stream.
  • Host DSP filters, demodulates, synchronizes, and decodes. GNU Radio lives here.

Transmit is the same chain in reverse: bits → symbols → DAC → mixer → filter → power amplifier → antenna. Most low-cost SDRs are receive-only or half-duplex. That is a safety feature, not a limitation to work around.

Sample rate is width, not tuning range

A complex stream at 2.4 Msps can represent about 2.4 MHz of spectrum, centred on the tuner frequency. It does not mean the radio can tune from 24 MHz to 1.7 GHz at once. The hardware tuner sets the centre; the sample rate sets the window.

Bandwidth is physics; channel width is law. Bandwidth is how much spectrum the signal actually occupies. Channel width is the allocation the regulator defines. A clean transmitter fits inside its channel; an overdriven or malfunctioning one spills into its neighbours.

I/Q is how software sees radio

A complex sample carries amplitude and phase. That sounds academic until you realise it is the difference between a spectrum with images and a spectrum you can actually demodulate.

~45 minrx only3 checkpoints

Tap each chip when you finish that phase.

01 Study / Two numbers, one signal

A real sample is one voltage. A complex sample is two: in-phase (I) and quadrature (Q), usually written z = I + jQ. GNU Radio’s native stream type is complex float32.

  • Magnitude is sqrt(I² + Q²): how strong the signal is.
  • Phase is atan2(Q, I): where the rotating vector points.
  • A positive-frequency tone rotates one way; a negative-frequency tone rotates the other way. This is why a complex FFT is not symmetric.
  • Real sampling must fold the spectrum and creates an image. Quadrature mixing uses two local oscillators 90° apart to reject that image.
  • Real hardware is imperfect. DC offset appears as a spike at the centre; I/Q imbalance creates a mirror image. Calibration and good gain staging reduce both.

The complex plane is also the constellation diagram. Once you understand I/Q, BPSK, QPSK, and QAM stop looking like arbitrary pictures and become points in the same plane.

Mental model

Think of a complex sample as a arrow with a length and a direction. Amplitude modulation changes the length. Phase modulation changes the direction. Frequency is how fast the direction changes.

Sampling, FFT, and the decibel

Most “the signal disappeared” bugs are actually sample-rate, resolution, or unit errors. Learn the three numbers that explain almost every display: sample rate, FFT size, and bandwidth.

~55 minrx only3 checkpoints

Tap each chip when you finish that phase.

01 Study / The numbers behind the picture

Nyquist: a real signal needs a sample rate above twice its highest frequency. A complex signal has a unique span of one sample rate, usually shown from −fs/2 to +fs/2. Anything outside that span aliases into it.

FFT: the display divides the sample rate into N bins. The bin width, often called resolution bandwidth, is fs / N. A larger FFT gives finer frequency resolution but a longer time window and more processing.

  • Window: reduces spectral leakage at the cost of smearing. Blackman-Harris is a good default for weak signals; rectangular is sharper but leakier.
  • Averaging: stabilises the noise floor but can hide short bursts. Never average while hunting a burst.
  • dB: a ratio. +3 dB is about 2× power; +10 dB is 10× power; +20 dB is 10× amplitude.
  • dBm: absolute power relative to 1 mW. 0 dBm = 1 mW. dBFS is relative to the ADC’s full scale, not the same thing.
Noise floor, quickly

Thermal noise is about −174 dBm/Hz at room temperature. Add the receiver noise figure and 10 log10(bandwidth). A 20 kHz channel with a 6 dB noise figure has a floor near −125 dBm. A bigger FFT shows a lower number per bin because each bin is narrower, not because the receiver became magical.

ADC bits: the ideal signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio is about 6.02N + 1.76 dB. An 8-bit ADC gives roughly 50 dB theoretical; real radios do worse. This is why a strong signal can bury a weak one even when the display looks “clean.”

GNU Radio anatomy

GRC is a graph editor that generates Python. The runtime is C++ blocks moving typed streams. Once you see the graph as backpressure and metadata, debugging gets much less mysterious.

~50 minsimulation3 checkpoints

Tap each chip when you finish that phase.

01 Study / The graph, the stream, the scheduler

A flowgraph is a set of blocks connected by typed ports. Data moves as streams of items; the scheduler moves buffers and applies backpressure. A block does not process “the signal” in one go; it processes chunks.

  • Source: produces samples. Hardware sources are paced by the radio; simulated sources are not.
  • Sink: consumes samples. GUI, audio, file, null, and network sinks are all consumers.
  • Sync block: one output item per input item. Decimator: fewer outputs. Interpolator: more outputs. Resampler: rational rate change.
  • Variable: a named value used by expressions. Change it in one place so rates stay consistent.
  • Data types: complex, float, int, short, byte. The colours and port shapes are not decoration; a mismatch is a hard error.

Throttle is the classic beginner trap. A simulated source can produce samples as fast as the CPU allows. Without a throttle, the flowgraph can starve the GUI, spin a core, or make audio collapse. Hardware sources already have a clock, so do not add a throttle unless you have a specific reason.

Read the generated Python

Press Generate, open the .py file, and find self.connect(...). The graphical graph is a convenience; the generated Python is the actual program. If you can read the connections, you can debug the graph.

03 Solo check / Close the notes

No-help question

Why does a simulated flowgraph usually need a Throttle, while an RTL-SDR flowgraph usually does not? What are you actually protecting?

Reveal answer

A simulated source has no real-time clock; it can produce items as fast as the scheduler can run. A throttle paces the stream so CPU, GUI, audio, and file writes are not overwhelmed. An SDR source is paced by the hardware and USB transfer rate, so adding a throttle is usually redundant.

You are protecting the runtime, not the signal. The samples are already valid; the problem is timing and resource pressure.

Your first receiver: FM broadcast

This is the “hello world” of SDR: a real antenna, a real signal, and a complete chain from RF to audio. Build it twice — once from a simulated FM signal, once from the air.

~60 minrx only3 checkpoints

Tap each chip when you finish that phase.

01 Study / From carrier to sound

FM broadcast lives around 88–108 MHz. It is wideband FM: roughly 200 kHz per station, 75 kHz deviation, with a 19 kHz stereo pilot and often RDS data at 57 kHz. A mono receiver ignores the stereo subcarrier and still recovers the audio.

Antenna→RTL-SDR→complex baseband→WBFM Receive→rational resampler→Audio Sink
  • Tuning: put the station at the centre of the captured window. A 2.4 Msps window is generous; 1 Msps is enough for one station plus margin.
  • WBFM Receive: internally does FM discrimination, decimation, and de-emphasis. Its output rate is quadrature_rate / audio_decimation.
  • Audio rate: 48 kHz is the common sound-card rate. Convert to it deliberately; do not hope the audio sink resamples for you.
  • Gain: start moderate. Too much RF gain on a strong station causes overload and distorted audio, not better reception.

Filters and resampling

Every rate change is a filter problem. Decimate without filtering and the spectrum folds back into your channel. This module makes aliasing visible instead of theoretical.

~50 minsimulation3 checkpoints

Tap each chip when you finish that phase.

01 Study / Shape, then change rate

A filter is a frequency-dependent gain. A low-pass filter keeps low frequencies and rejects high ones. The important parameters are cutoff, transition width, stopband attenuation, and the number of taps. A narrow transition costs more taps and more CPU.

  • Decimation keeps one sample out of every N. It lowers the rate and the bandwidth, but only after an anti-alias low-pass filter.
  • Interpolation inserts samples and creates spectral images. It must be followed by a low-pass filter; a rational resampler can include those taps. Skipping the filter puts the images on the air.
  • Rational resampling changes by interp/decim; use it for rates like 50 kHz → 48 kHz (24/25) or 200 kHz → 48 kHz (6/25).
  • Group delay is real. Filters shift the signal in time; this matters for burst timing and packet sync.
  • Complex filters can be asymmetric and select positive or negative frequencies; real filters are symmetric.
Rule of thumb

Filter first, then decimate. If you can reduce the rate early, do it — but only after removing the energy that would alias into the new band. Cheap rates are a reward for correct filtering, not a shortcut around it.

Gain, noise, and dynamic range

“Turn up the gain” is not a debugging strategy. Gain changes the noise floor, the signal, and the distortion together. Learn to tell which one you are actually hearing.

~45 minhardware preferred3 checkpoints

Tap each chip when you finish that phase.

01 Study / More gain is not more sensitivity

An SDR has several gain stages: RF/LNA, mixer/IF, and baseband. Each stage adds noise and has a maximum level. The goal is to put the wanted signal high enough above the noise without driving any stage into compression.

  • Noise figure is how much noise the receiver adds. Lower is better. The first stage dominates.
  • Sensitivity is the weakest signal you can detect at a given bandwidth and SNR. It is not a single number.
  • Dynamic range is the span between the noise floor and the overload point. 8-bit radios have less than 12-bit radios.
  • Intermodulation happens when strong signals mix in a nonlinear stage and create products at new frequencies.
  • AGC hides level changes. For measurements, turn it off and record the gain.
  • RSSI is relative and antenna-dependent. It is not a distance sensor.

Gain has a specific job: use the ADC’s full scale. If the signal is tiny relative to full scale, only a few ADC levels describe it, so effective resolution and SNR suffer. If gain is too high, the waveform clips and the ADC stops measuring it faithfully. The sweet spot is enough gain for good bit usage without compression, clipping, or new intermodulation products.

Cyber lens / denial and deception

A strong nearby transmitter can desensitise a receiver or create false products. This is a physical-layer denial-of-service condition. A defender needs front-end filtering, gain control, and a baseline that distinguishes “new signal” from “my receiver is being overloaded.”

Reveal answer

No. If both rise equally, the ratio is unchanged. Gain only helps when the signal rises more than the noise — typically when the receiver’s own noise was dominating the input. Past that point, gain just pushes the same SNR into a more nonlinear region.

For a strong nearby signal, an attenuator can help by preventing front-end compression, even though it reduces the signal. The goal is linearity, not maximum trace height.

Analog demodulation

AM, FM, and SSB are different ways of putting a message on a carrier. The demodulator is a decision about which property of the wave carries the information.

~55 minsimulation + rx3 checkpoints

Tap each chip when you finish that phase.

01 Study / Three ways to carry a message

  • AM: the message changes the carrier’s amplitude. It has a carrier and two sidebands, so a 5 kHz audio signal needs about 10 kHz of RF bandwidth. Simple, but sensitive to amplitude noise and fading.
  • FM: the message changes the carrier’s frequency. The envelope stays constant, which makes it more resistant to amplitude noise above the threshold. Carson’s rule gives an approximate bandwidth: B ≈ 2(Δf + f_m). Broadcast FM also pre-emphasises high audio frequencies before transmission and de-emphasises them at the receiver; this improves SNR without making the audio sound bright.
  • SSB: one sideband, carrier suppressed. It is efficient in bandwidth and power, but requires frequency and phase accuracy. Ham operators use it heavily.
  • CW: a carrier switched on and off. It is not “digital” just because the key is a switch; it is still an analog carrier.

Demodulation is about recovering the message from amplitude, phase, or frequency. An envelope detector is just a magnitude operation. A quadrature demodulator differentiates phase. A PLL tracks phase or frequency. Audio filtering and de-emphasis shape the result so it sounds right.

Security reality

Analog modulation has no confidentiality or integrity. Anyone with a receiver can demodulate it. The only protections are legal, physical, or at a higher layer. “It sounds like noise” is not encryption.

Digital modulation and symbol recovery

Digital is still analog underneath. Bits become symbols, symbols become waveforms, and the receiver has to recover carrier, timing, and frame boundaries. That is where most real decoders live or die.

~70 minsimulation3 checkpoints

Tap each chip when you finish that phase.

01 Study / Bits do not travel as bits

A digital transmitter maps bits to symbols, shapes the symbols into a waveform, and upconverts. The receiver must undo each step. The ratio sps = sample_rate / symbol_rate is the number of samples per symbol; real receivers usually want several samples per symbol for timing recovery.

  • OOK/ASK: amplitude on/off or multilevel. Simple remotes and sensors use it. Envelope detection works, but noise and interference look like bits.
  • FSK: frequency shifts. Noncoherent discriminator or coherent detector. Common in telemetry, remotes, and IoT.
  • BPSK/QPSK: phase shifts. Needs carrier recovery; a Costas loop is the usual tool. More spectral efficiency, more sync complexity. PSK is phase modulation with a discrete alphabet; FM is the continuous-phase relative, where frequency is the derivative of phase.
  • QAM: amplitude and phase. Higher throughput, tighter SNR and linearity requirements.
  • Pulse shaping: a root-raised-cosine filter limits bandwidth and reduces intersymbol interference. The receiver uses a matched filter.
  • Timing recovery: finds the best sampling instant. Gardner, Mueller-Muller, and the generic Symbol Sync block are common.
  • Frame sync: a correlator finds a known preamble or access code. A sync word is a filter, not authentication.
Cyber lens / sync is not trust

Anyone can transmit the same preamble. A correlator that locks on a sync word proves only that the bits look like the protocol. It does not prove who sent them, whether they are fresh, or whether the payload is authorized.

MODULE 10 / PHASE III · DIGITAL

Framing, checksums, and packets

Bits are not a message. A protocol defines where a message starts, how long it is, what it means, and whether it arrived intact. This is where RF reverse engineering becomes software analysis.

~55 minmessage domain3 checkpoints

Tap each chip when you finish that phase.

01 Study / A packet is a contract

A stream has no inherent message boundaries. Framing adds them. A typical packet has a preamble, a sync word, a length or type field, an address, a payload, and a checksum or FEC. Some protocols whiten the payload to balance the spectrum; some use Manchester or PWM line coding.

preamble→sync→length / type→address→payload→CRC / FEC
  • CRC detects accidental errors. It is not a MAC and not a signature. An attacker can recompute it.
  • Whitening prevents long runs of identical bits and shapes the spectrum; it is reversible, not secret.
  • Bit order and endianness are protocol facts. MSB-first versus LSB-first will make a correct capture look like garbage.
  • Rolling codes add freshness to prevent simple replay. They do not stop a real-time relay.
  • FEC corrects errors, but it also gives an attacker more chances to inject a valid-looking frame.
Cyber lens / checksums are not authentication

CRC-16, CRC-32, and a fixed sync word are integrity hints for noisy links, not proof of origin. If the protocol has no key, no nonce, and no authenticated MAC, anyone who can transmit can forge a valid frame.

No-help question

Reveal answer

Yes. A CRC is a public function of the bytes; the attacker can build a payload and compute the correct CRC. The sync word only helps the receiver find the frame. Neither provides authenticity or freshness.

A rolling code prevents replaying an old captured code, but a relay forwards the live challenge and response between the legitimate key and the car. The code is fresh; the distance is the lie. Defeating relay requires distance bounding or another physical/secure element control, not just a counter.

01 Study / Extend the graph without fighting it

import numpy as np
from gnuradio import gr

class blk(gr.sync_block):
    def __init__(self):
        gr.sync_block.__init__(
            self,
            name='burst gate',
            in_sig=[np.complex64],
            out_sig=[np.complex64],
        )

    def work(self, input_items, output_items):
        x = input_items[0]
        y = output_items[0]
        y[:] = np.where(np.abs(x) > 0.1, x, 0)
        return len(y)

01 Study / Waves, bands, and the antenna

Radio is an electromagnetic wave: an electric field and a magnetic field oscillating perpendicular to each other and travelling at the speed of light. Frequency is how many cycles pass per second; wavelength is the distance between cycles:

λ = c / f

λ = wavelength in metres
c = 299,792,458 m/s (use 3×10⁸ for estimates)
f = frequency in hertz

At 100 MHz, one wavelength is about 3 metres. At 433.92 MHz it is about 69 cm. At 2.4 GHz it is about 12.5 cm. That is why low-frequency antennas are large and microwave antennas are small.

Propagation is not one thing

  • Line of sight: VHF/UHF generally behaves like light; hills and buildings cast shadows. The radio horizon is usually farther than the optical horizon because of atmospheric refraction.
  • Multipath: reflections arrive with different delays and phases. They can add or cancel; this is fading.
  • Fresnel zone: the path is not a razor-thin line. Obstructions near the path can still hurt even when the antennas “see” each other.
  • HF skywave: the ionosphere can refract HF over the horizon. It is time-of-day, season, and solar-weather dependent.
  • Penetration: lower frequencies generally penetrate walls better; higher frequencies give more bandwidth but less coverage.

Bandwidth vs channel width

Bandwidth is a property of the signal: how much spectrum it actually occupies. Channel width is a human and legal allocation: the slot the signal is allowed to occupy. A clean transmitter fits inside its channel; a malfunctioning or overdriven one splatters into neighbours.

Antennas are impedance-matched resonators

  • A quarter-wave monopole is roughly λ/4 long; a half-wave dipole is roughly λ/2 overall.
  • Gain is measured in dBi, relative to an isotropic radiator. It is not free power; it is directionality.
  • Polarization matters. A vertically polarized receive antenna is best for a vertically polarized transmitter; mismatch costs signal.
  • Most SDRs and antennas are designed for 50 Ω. A mismatch causes reflection, loss, and sometimes damage at high transmit power.
  • Feedline loss is real. A long cheap cable can cost more than the antenna gain adds.

No-help question

What are the wavelength, quarter-wave, and half-wave dimensions at 100 MHz? Why is a 50 Ω match important, and why can two antennas with the same gain behave differently?

Reveal answer

At 100 MHz, λ = 300/100 = 3 m; quarter-wave is about 0.75 m; half-wave is about 1.5 m. A 50 Ω match minimises reflection and loss between the antenna, feedline, and radio. SDR hardware under the hood

The flowgraph sees complex samples. The radio sees a mixer, filters, amplifiers, ADCs, a clock, and a bus. Understanding that chain explains gain, clipping, DC spikes, overflow, and why “more sample rate” is not always better.

~65 minrx only3 checkpoints

01 Study / From antenna to IQ

Antenna→RF filter / gain→mixer + LO→IF→I/Q mixers→anti-alias LPF→BB gain→ADC→USB / host

The mixer translates RF down to a lower frequency. The I/Q pair preserves the sign of the offset; the ADCs digitise each component. An analog low-pass filter before the ADC removes energy above Nyquist so it cannot alias into your capture.

  • Capture window: the complex bandwidth equals the sample rate and is centred on the LO/tuned frequency.
  • Resolution: an N-bit ADC has 2^N levels. More bits means finer quantisation and more theoretical dynamic range.
  • Full scale and clipping: gain is useful until the ADC input hits its maximum. Then the waveform is clipped and distortion appears.
  • Overflow: if the host cannot process samples fast enough, samples are dropped. Audio glitches, gaps, or “O”/“U” messages are clues.
  • DC offset: a false spike at 0 Hz from analogue imperfections, not a transmitter at the centre frequency.
  • Gain stages: RF, IF, and baseband gain are not interchangeable. RF gain is broadband and can overload or damage the front end; IF/BB gain can distort if pushed too far.

03 Solo check / Close the notes

No-help question

Why does an 8-bit SDR have less theoretical dynamic range than a 12-bit SDR? Why is a DC spike at the centre not a signal? Why can a higher sample rate make things worse?

Reveal answer

An 8-bit ADC has 256 levels; a 12-bit ADC has 4,096. More levels mean finer quantisation and a lower quantisation-noise floor, so the theoretical SNR rises by about 6 dB per bit. The DC spike is an offset in the analogue chain and an FFT artefact at 0 Hz; it moves with the tuned centre and is not RF energy from a transmitter. A higher sample rate widens the capture window but increases USB/host load and can cause overflow, so the flowgraph may drop samples or distort.

Peripherals, noise, and the bench

The SDR is one part of the radio. The antenna, feedline, connectors, filters, power, and even the laptop charger can decide whether you hear a signal or a wall of noise.

~55 minrx only3 checkpoints

Tap each chip when you finish that phase.

01 Study / The rest of the radio

Antenna

Whip, dipole, loop, Yagi

A whip is a broadband omnidirectional monopole; a dipole trades bandwidth for pattern; a loop is compact for low frequencies; a Yagi is directional and high-gain. Match the antenna to the band and the direction.

Feedline

Connectors and adapters

SMA, RP-SMA, BNC, N, MCX, and SO-239 are not interchangeable. Every adapter adds loss and a failure point. Keep the chain short and use 50 Ω parts.

Front end

LNA, filter, attenuator, limiter

An LNA helps weak signals if it is before the loss and not overloaded. A filter rejects out-of-band energy. An attenuator protects the receiver. A limiter caps dangerous power.

Power

Bias tee and upconverter

A bias tee puts DC on the coax for an active antenna or LNA. An upconverter shifts low frequencies into the SDR’s tuning range. A downconverter/LNB does the reverse for high frequencies.

Noise

Computer and charger EMI

USB power, switch-mode chargers, displays, and CPUs radiate noise. Battery power, ferrites, shielded cables, short USB runs, and antenna placement often help more than a better SDR.

Bench

Toolkit discipline

Known-good antenna, adapters, 10/20/30 dB attenuators, dummy load, ferrites, labelled cables, and a notebook. Change one variable at a time.

Noise mitigation checklist

  • Run the laptop on battery and compare the noise floor with the charger connected.
  • Add a ferrite choke to the USB cable and the power cable.
  • Use a shielded feedline; keep it away from displays and power supplies.
  • Move the antenna away from the computer and use a USB extension.
  • Rotate a directional antenna to maximise signal-to-noise, not just signal.
  • Terminate unused ports and avoid adapters that are not needed.

Source material: Practical SDR, chapter 12.

03 Solo check / Close the notes

No-help question

Why can a ferrite choke improve reception? Why should an LNA go near the antenna rather than after a long lossy cable? Why is RP-SMA not the same as SMA?

Reveal answer

A ferrite suppresses common-mode RF current on the cable, reducing noise coupled from the computer and power supply. An LNA early in the chain amplifies the weak signal before the cable’s loss and before the receiver’s noise dominates; putting it after a lossy cable amplifies both signal and noise and may not improve SNR. RP-SMA is reverse-polarity SMA: the centre pin and threaded shell are swapped to prevent antenna swaps on some Wi-Fi gear. It will not mate correctly with standard SMA without an adapter.

Transmit chain and legal boundaries

Transmitting is the receive chain backwards, with more ways to break the law and damage hardware. Learn the chain, the filtering, and the safety rules before you key anything.

~65 minsoftware loopback only3 checkpoints

Tap each chip when you finish that phase.

01 Study / The reverse chain

bits / audio→modulator→interpolate→low-pass→upconvert→DAC→IF/RF gain→PA + filter→antenna
  • Interpolation creates images. Filter after interpolation, before upconversion; a rational resampler can include the low-pass taps.
  • Harmonics and splatter are the transmitter’s version of aliasing. A nonlinear PA or overdriven DAC puts energy into adjacent channels.
  • Sample scaling matters. SDR sinks expect roughly −1 to +1 per I/Q component. Too much digital gain clips before the signal reaches the RF stage.
  • TX gain stages are not a volume knob. Start low, increase only as needed, and verify with a receiver or test equipment.
  • Duty cycle and power are often linked in unlicensed rules. Lower duty cycle may allow higher peak power; continuous transmission usually does not.
  • Half-duplex radios cannot receive while transmitting. The receiver is blind during the transmission, and the antenna switch must be controlled.
Authorization first

Never connect a transmitter to an antenna without a licence or written authorization. Use a dummy load and shielded enclosure for bench tests. Do not jam, spoof, or transmit on a band you do not own or have permission to use. When in doubt, do not transmit.

No-help question

Why should you filter after interpolation? Why do harmonics matter even when the fundamental is clean? Why is a dummy load safer than an antenna for a bench test?

Reveal answer

Interpolation inserts samples and creates spectral images; a low-pass filter removes the images before they reach the upconverter and PA. Harmonics are real transmissions at multiples of the fundamental and can interfere with other services even if the fundamental is inside its channel. A dummy load converts RF energy to heat instead of radiating it, so you can test the chain without polluting the spectrum or exceeding your authorization.

Accuracy posture

  • GNU Radio block names and core simulated chains were checked against GNU Radio 3.10.9.2 in this environment. Labels and parameters can differ in other versions.
  • The noise-floor tool uses the standard first-order −174 dBm/Hz + NF + 10 log10(B) model; it ignores antenna noise, interference, ADC behaviour, and front-end nonlinearity.
  • The link budget is free-space only. It ignores terrain, Fresnel obstruction, multipath, polarization mismatch, cable loss, and antenna patterns.
  • The constellations are ideal AWGN models. They do not include phase noise, carrier offset, timing error, ISI, or nonlinear distortion.
  • Hardware ranges are typical, not guarantees. Always confirm your specific radio, antenna, and local regulations.
  • Modules 16–19 are expanded from Practical SDR (Clark & Clark, 2025). HackRF-specific values such as the 0/14 dB RF gain stage are examples, not universal SDR specs.

Legal checklist

Before you capture: confirm the band is legal to receive in your jurisdiction; confirm you are not intercepting private communications; confirm storage and sharing comply with privacy law. Before you transmit: hold a licence or written authorization; use a dummy load/shield; verify power, frequency, bandwidth, and filters; document the test. When in doubt, do not transmit.
